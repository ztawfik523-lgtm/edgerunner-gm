# Writing documents

Write for what the reader needs to understand, decide or do. Keep document structure and detail proportionate to that purpose.

## Compose around substance

- Structure the document around the reader's questions, leading with the result, proposal or explanation they need.
- Identify requirements, proposals, examples, facts and open choices clearly. A document-level status statement often suffices; qualify individual claims where their status or certainty differs.
- When revising, integrate changes into the existing explanation and update affected references.

## Clear prose

- Lead with the result, proposal, or explanation the reader needs.
- Use concrete claims and specific evidence. Keep uncertainty where it affects the claim; don't add vague attribution or confidence beyond the evidence.
- Avoid stock openers, inflated significance, filler conclusions, repetitive signposting, and templated contrasts when they add no meaning. These are editing cues, not banned phrases or proof of AI authorship.
- Vary sentence and paragraph shape where the ideas call for it. Don't force symmetry, lists, or dramatic fragments onto material that doesn't need them.
- State an idea directly. Use contrast or negation when it carries a real distinction; preserve meaningful caveats and scope.

## Proposals and plans

- Separate established requirements, decisions, proposals, examples, and unresolved choices.
- Describe concrete needs before mechanisms. Match process, safeguards, and detail to real requirements and credible risks.
- Explain material costs, dependencies, and trade-offs. Treat speculative extensions as options, not commitments.
- Investigate enough to make a sound recommendation, then move approved work forward. Escalate choices only when they materially affect the outcome.

## Documentation maintenance

- Keep maintained information in one authoritative home; link supporting documents where useful.
- On revision, integrate changes into the existing explanation and update affected references. Remove obsolete or duplicated guidance.
- Keep evidence and examples that help readers understand or act; omit routine implementation history better carried by code or version control.

For task instructions and agent handoffs, use the `prompt-authoring` skill. For research reports, use `research-synthesis` to gather and qualify evidence. For targeted editing of an existing draft, use `prose-editing`.
